What this appendix is

The course asks for the estimation methods used, the ranges produced and the reasoning behind them. This page records them in the order the team produced them. Part 1 is the team's completed worksheet, "A First Look at Your Project's Size", which takes the outside view: the team's own experience and three similar products, as comparators for analogous estimation. Part 2 is the team's completed activity "Estimate One Slice of Your Project": one slice of the product, estimated by function points and by analogy, with story points as a cross-check, and the range a sponsor reads.

The entries are carried here as the team wrote them. In Part 1, two columns were tightened for the page: the evidence column cites the records in the interview evidence that each capability rests on, and each date in the comparators is matched to the listed source that states it, with the gaps marked. Part 2 is the team's submitted version of 30 September 2026, carried as submitted; the revision history below lists how it got there. The worksheet and the activity as delivered are the Team's PDFs above.

Three real products that solve a problem close to GPI's, at least one small and at least one big, measured from the start of development to the first public release. Under each entry, a source check notes which of the listed sources states each figure.

Product A, small: Upcoming.org

FieldEntry
Product and companyUpcoming.org, a community event calendar started by Andy Baio as a side project. Bought by Yahoo! in 2005.
Why it is similar to GPIA city-by-city listing of what is happening, so people hear about events before they happen. The same job as GPI, built by one person with no budget.
Development startedJanuary 2003 (notes from Baio's talk "A Tale of Three .Orgs," PDX Web Innovators, 4 June 2008; no Baio-written source for the start date, so treat it as secondhand)
First public release19 September 2003 (Baio's own launch post on waxy.org)
Time to first releaseAbout 9 months, worked nights and mornings around a full-time job. Baio's own verdict: "Rough site launched. Barely functional."
What the first release includedAnyone could add events they were going to, in fixed categories; see who else was going; browse by metro area; a friends list; RSS feeds. No API, tagging, email or SMS until March 2005.
Team sizeOne at launch. Baio kept his day job until the acquisition; two partners joined later.
What workedSupply came from attendees posting what they were going to, not from promoters. Self-promotion was "banned entirely for its first two years." A city did not need broad coverage to be useful: "only 2 or 3 individuals" were enough to make a college town work.
What did not workAfter the founders left Yahoo! at the end of 2007, "the social features that made Upcoming unique were minimized, or removed entirely," and "spam, like creeping kudzu, was left unchecked." By 2009 "the only people using Upcoming were event promoters and spammers." A Kickstarter-funded rebuild funded 30 May 2014 did not relaunch until March 2017, about 34 months.
Sourceswaxy.org/2003/09/upcomingorg_lau/; waxy.org/2013/04/the_death_of_upcomingorg/; stubborndreams.me/2008/07/31/a-brief-history-of-upcoming-dot-org-by-andy-baio/; en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upcoming

Source check. The January 2003 start, the nine months, the nights and mornings around a day job, "only 2 or 3 individuals" and the October 2005 acquisition are in the 2008 write-up of Baio's talk. The 19 September 2003 launch is Baio's own launch post, confirmed by Wikipedia. The end-of-2007 departure, the minimized social features, the spam and the 2009 line are in Baio's 2013 post. Step 4: what the comparison says

  1. Which hidden-work letter appears most often, and did the similar products struggle with it? Data (D) appears the most often, which can be seen in all 8 capabilities. Do512 also struggled with static data which led to inaccurate and outdated listings. Upcoming.org also struggled with data quality and moderation, eventually dying out when unmoderated spam ruined the listings.
  2. How long did the similar products take, with how many people, and what does that suggest? Upcoming.org took roughly 9 months for a first release, with only one person. Do512.com took 1 month for the first release; initially it was two people, they then recruited two more people. Fever took about two years for the first release; there were four co-founders for the first release. Although we deployed a functional baseline website in one week after doing research, our comparables indicate that developing a production-ready first release would take about 6 to 12 months.
  3. One practice to copy and one mistake to avoid. Practice to copy: allow external users and organizers to directly submit and manage event content. Mistake to avoid: avoid static listings since it leads to inaccuracy and outdated data.

Our comparables suggest a first release would take 6 to 12 months with a team of 5.

The team's completed activity "Estimate One Slice of Your Project" (Lesson 9, Part 2), carried here as the team submitted it on 30 September 2026. The event supply follows the charter and the strategy: organizers post, the team curates at first, and no outside-source import is in the slice, so both methods estimate the same functions. The figures stay provisional on the rates: the function-point productivity rate and the analogous inputs are assumed, not measured. The team confirmed the seven story point values and verified the wage figure on 29 September 2026.

Step 2: the two methods

Analogous and function points. In the team's words: "Analogous method begins at the market research level where we identify facts such as failures and successes of a product. We can prove through research and facts about how an organization might have shipped a similar product and succeeded or failed. Function points, on the other hand, start from one's own design indicating count of inputs, results, lookups and data in our slices. Neither method uses the other's numbers, so if they agree, the agreement means something." Use-case points were not used. Story points (3C) were completed as a cross-check, not as a declared method.

The fraction moves the answer by about 40 percent across its range and the uplift by about 8 percent; the hours per person-month move it without limit: at 100 hours per person-month the range would be 563 to 851 hours, and at a full-time 160, 900 to 1,361.

Gap: larger middle value ÷ smaller middle value = 639 ÷ 491 = 1.30, an absolute difference of 148 hours.

  1. Do the two methods depend on the same assumption? No, the function points method starts from our internal design counts (inputs, reports, results, etc.) in Step 1, multiplied by productivity rates. The analogous method starts from external market historical data scaled to our scope and adjusted for 5-person team coordination costs.
  2. Which single input moves the answer most? The productivity rate in function points moves the estimate by 426 hours, from 426 at 6 h/FP to 852 at 12. In the analogous method, holding the coordination uplift fixed at 30 percent and moving the matched-release fraction from 50 to 70 percent takes the estimate from 409.5 to 573.3 hours, a difference of 163.8 hours. The full 394 to 595 range varies the uplift as well, between 1.25 and 1.35, so that wider 201.6 hour spread cannot be attributed to the fraction alone. The analogous method's lever is the 70 hours per person-month assumed for Baio's side project: at 100 hours per person-month the range would be 563 to 851 hours, and at a full-time 160, 900 to 1,361 hours, more than the fraction or the uplift can move it.
  3. What information would narrow the gap, and when could you get it? Our first one or two actual software-development sprints would give early effort observations, but they would not establish a reliable rate. We need hours recorded against completed stories, and Slide 26 sets at least three completed sprints by the same team before velocity means anything. Course-portal documentation sprints do not count as GPI development velocity. More data should narrow the 6 to 12 hour range; it will not eliminate the uncertainty.

The team's explanation of the gap. Function point yielded a middle value of 639 hours, whereas the Analogous method yielded a middle value of 491 hours, creating a gap of 1.30x (under 2.0x). The primary driver of the gap is that our Function point estimate assumes a wide productivity rate of 6 to 12 hours per point due to lack of sprint data. Meanwhile, the Analogous method relies on our assumed Fraction of their release our slice matches and a 70-hour per person month. Both methods start from completely independent information, so the gap represents the distance between two assumptions we have never tested. The optional story-point cross-check gives 463 to 925 hours for the team's confirmed 37-point slice. Its result of 925 hours exceeds the two-method upper bound of 852 hours by 73 hours. Because the cross-check velocity is an untested assumption and story points are not a declared estimation method, this indicates upper-end uncertainty rather than automatically changing the formal range. What would narrow the gap is real hours logged against finished stories. Holding the coordination uplift at 30 percent and moving the feature match from 50 to 70 percent takes the analogous estimate from 409.5 to 573.3 hours, a swing of 163.8 hours. The wider 394 to 595 spread varies the uplift as well, so the fraction alone does not account for all of it.
