## Estimate, budget, baseline

Three different things. The Session 9 estimate becomes a budget only when someone with authority approves it, and the baseline is the version the team is held to.

| | Estimate | Budget | Cost baseline |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A forecast of effort and cost, with a range | The money leadership approves, including a cushion for surprises | The approved month-by-month spending plan you measure against |
| Set by | The project team | The sponsor (the executive who pays) | Built by the project manager, approved by the sponsor |
| GPI | 394 to 852 hours = $32,377 to $70,014. Expected 603.67 hours = $49,607 (traced to Step 3 in this document) | Requested: $58,868. Baseline: $56,065. Cushion: $2,803 (Step 10) | $56,065: the $49,622 base build phased over 3 months (M1 $17,820, M2 $17,805, M3 $13,997), plus $6,443 contingency held back for named risks (Step 10) |

## Two ways to start a budget

| | Top-down | Bottom-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it starts | From a number someone wants, such as a fixed date or a fixed dollar amount. | From the list of work that has to be done, priced piece by piece. |
| GPI | [Project Charter](/sprint-1/project-charter/): 8 weeks × 50 team-hours = 400 hours × $82.18 (per productive hour) ≈ $32,870 | Seven work packages (37 points, 71 function points): 603.67 hours, $49,607 of labor plus $15 non-labor = $49,622 |
| Use it for | A quick reality check. It tells you what leadership hopes for. | The budget you defend. It tells you what the work costs. |

Build bottom-up, then set it beside the top-down number. The gap between the two is a finding, written up in Check 5 under Step 10.

## Step 1: what the cost depends on

A cost-driving assumption is a statement that, if it turned out wrong, would change the cost. The team lists five, each with an owner.

| Assumption | Why it drives cost | Owner |

| Organizers post events into GPI-created catalogue, and the team curates it first, like how the charter and Business Strategy have it. | Rules out imported work in the build and sets the curation hours in the operating cost. | Oscar Vargas, Business Strategy Lead |

See the notes below. | Sets the 12.1-week expectation and the monthly spend. Fewer hours contributed extends the calendar, but total hours remain the same. Pricing project management on top would add about 58.5 hours ($4,807). | Jazmin Huerta, Market Research Lead |
| 6 to 12 hours per function point, 70 hours per person-month for the comparable, with a 50 to 70 percent match and a 25 to 35 percent uplift. We have not measured any of these yet. | Our whole range depends on these. After three sprints we'll replace them with our real numbers. | Christian Lopez, Research Documentation Lead (Appendix owner) |
| Labor at $51.36 an hour (ZipRecruiter) = $106,829 a year. × 1.20 burden = $10,683 per FTE-month, or $82.18 per productive hour. Tools and hosting stay on free tiers, since the charter has no money for paid licenses. See the notes below. | Every dollar amount is the number of hours times this rate. A 1.4 burden would push labor up 17 percent, and paying to host the app would add a new cost. | Samuel Alderete, Portal Lead |

<Callout title="Notes to rows 3 and 5">
The 65/17/10/8 percentages are shares of the delivery hours (545.17), not of the full 603.67. The 0.15 is an FTE figure, not a percentage of hours. It means 15% of one full-time person each month, which is 0.15 × 130 = 19.5 hours a month, or 58.5 hours over 3 months. This is how the hours are carved out:

1. Start with the total: 603.67 hours.
2. Take the PM's share off first: 603.67 − 58.5 = 545.17 hours left for delivery.
3. Split those 545.17 hours 65/17/10/8 among the delivery roles.

The Session 9 estimate priced hours at $61.63, a loaded rate per paid hour; this budget prices them per productive hour, $82.18.
</Callout>

## Step 3: effort more than one way

### Three-point (PERT)

E = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6. O, the optimistic case, is 394 hours, if everything goes well. M, the most likely, is 594 hours. P, the pessimistic case, is 852 hours, if real problems hit. E = 3,622 ÷ 6 = 603.67 hours.

Why E is above M: the bad case is farther from most likely than the good case is, so it pulls the answer up. Spread = (P − O) ÷ 6 = (852 − 394) ÷ 6 = 76.33 hours: a typical miss. Step 9 reuses it.

### The methods side by side

| Method | Hours | What it starts from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bottom-up (the packages) | 603.67 | The seven work packages in Step 2 at 14.73 hours per story point across 37 points, plus 58.5 project management hours. The package hours were distributed from the PERT expected value, so this line is not an independent check on PERT. |
| Three-point (PERT) | 603.67 | O 394 from the analogous low. P 852 from 71 function points at 12 hours each. M 594 from the midpoint of the two method ranges (623) and the midpoint of their two middles (565). |
| Analogous | 491 | Upcoming.org: one person over about 9 months at an assumed 70 hours per person-month, so 630 hours, scaled by a 50 to 70 percent feature match and a 25 to 35 percent coordination uplift. Range 394 to 595. |
| **Planning value** | **603.67** | The PERT expected value, which the bottom-up packages plus project management also sum to. This is the number carried into Step 4. |

<Chart name="effort-estimates" />

**Do the methods land within 25 percent of each other?** The rule of thumb: if they differ by more than 25 percent, find out why before pricing anything. The team's answer: not needed, the spread is 23 percent. The planning value of 603.67 hours sits 22.9 percent above the 491 hour analogous midpoint, inside the 25 percent rule. Bottom-up and PERT are identical because the package hours were distributed from the PERT expected value, so analogous is the only independent comparison in the table.

How to read it: one FTE-month is one person working full time for one month. About 80 percent of a month (130 of 160 hours) is productive project time; the rest goes to meetings, email, support and time off. The rate the team assumed: 130 productive hours per FTE-month.

At 130 productive hours per FTE-month, 603.67 hours is 4.64 FTE-months of work. Our own Step 1 assumption is 10 productive hours per person per week, which is 50 team-hours a week, so those 4.64 FTE-months spread across about 12.1 weeks of calendar time. The role rows are rounded to two decimals; the total of 4.64 is taken from the unrounded values.

Project management is priced at 0.15 FTE for 3 months, about 4.5 hours a week, and is carved out of the 603.67-hour planning value.

## Step 9: the risks and the two reserves

### Price the risks: the contingency reserve

| Risk | Chance | Impact | EMV |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Effort lands near the pessimistic 852 hrs; our rates are not measured yet (+248.33 hrs) | 15% | $20,407 | $3,061 |
| Organizers don't post, so we import events from outside sources (108 hrs) | 30% | $8,875 | $2,663 |
| Uneven El Paso-Juárez event data needs a cleanup and review pass (35 hrs) | 25% | $2,876 | $719 |
| **Contingency reserve** | | | **$6,443** |

How to read it: expected monetary value (EMV) = chance × impact. It is an average, not a prediction: a risk either costs about its impact or nothing, and the reserve covers the expected total. The template asks for the top three risks at least.

Cross-check: one PERT spread from Step 3 × the productive-hour rate = $6,273 (76.33 hrs × $82.18) of effort risk alone. Is the reserve at least that large? Yes, $6,443 is $170 more.

### Five sanity checks

1. **Totals match.** Add the staffing plan by month and compare it to Step 4. Ours: 4.64 FTE-months by month (1.667 + 1.667 + 1.310) = 4.64 FTE-months in Step 4.
2. **Labor share is plausible.** Divide labor by the base build. Ours: $49,607 ÷ $49,622 = 99.97 percent. Nearly all cost is people because every tool and service stays on a free tier.
3. **Estimates agree.** Compare the effort estimates. Ours: 491 to 603.67 hours, within 23 percent.
4. **Reserves trace to risks.** Every contingency dollar links to a named risk in the Step 9 table. Ours: $3,061 + $2,663 + $719 = $6,443, all three named in the Step 9 table.
5. **Top-down comparison.** Put the sponsor's number next to yours and explain the gap. Sponsor's number: 400 hours (8 weeks × 50 team-hours), about $32,870 of labor. Ours: 603.67 hours, $49,607 of labor. The gap is 203.67 hours, or $16,737, about 51 percent more. Eight weeks at our 50 team-hours a week only fits 400 hours, but the seven stories in the slice price at 603.67 hours, which takes about 12.1 weeks at that pace. To close the gap either the slice gets smaller or the timeline gets longer.
